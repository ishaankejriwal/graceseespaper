Abstract Since 2002, the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and GRACE Follow‐On
(GRACE/‐FO) satellite missions have provided unprecedented measurements of terrestrial water storage changes (TWSC). These data are essential for monitoring the global water cycle, supporting drought and flood risk management, and informing water‐related decision‐making. However, GRACE products are typically released with a latency of several months, limiting their utility for real‐time and operational forecasting applications. In this study, we use machine learning to forecast GRACE‐like TWSC up to 12 months ahead, relying solely on observational and reanalysis‐based inputs. The observation‐driven forecast approach is evaluated over the period 2010–2024 and benchmarked against seasonal forecasts from the European Centre for Medium‐Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF)’s new long‐range forecasting system (SEAS5). Our results show that the developed method offers improved accuracy and robustness compared to the ECMWF forecasts, providing a viable data‐driven alternative for operational TWSC forecasting. We generate global forecast data sets at 1° resolution, creating a robust, publicly available resource that extends GRACE‐like insights into the near future. The study addresses the latency of GRACE/‐FO products by offering real‐time TWSC forecasts to support applications such as drought early warning, sea level prediction, hydrological model validation, and geodetic applications such as forecasting Earth orientation parameters via hydrological angular momentum excitation or estimating loading corrections in GNSS and altimetry data analysis. The hindcast data set (2010– 2024) evaluated in this study and the regularly updated semi‐operational forecast data set (from 2024 onward) are publicly available at: https://doi.pangaea.de/10.1594/PANGAEA.973113 and https://www.igg.uni‐bonn. de/apmg/de/data‐and‐models/grace‐fo‐forecasting.
1. Introduction
Total water storage (TWS) represents an integration of all water stored in land aquifers, including groundwater, surface water, soil moisture, snow, and glaciers (Rodell et al., 2018). Given its significance to the global hydrological cycle, TWS has been established as an Essential Climate Variable (ECV) (GCOS, 2022). Traditional methods for assessing total water storage change (TWSC) rely on accumulating individual water compartments (i. e., groundwater, surface water, etc.) simulated by hydrological and land surface models (Soltani et al., 2021). However, traditional models utilize only climate data as inputs, thereby failing to account for the impact of large‐scale human activities, such as irrigation, dam construction, and reservoir operations, on TWSC (Felfelani et al., 2017). Furthermore, existing land surface and hydrological models lack important process representations, such as adequate descriptions of soil percolation or evapotranspiration (Vereecken et al., 2022), leading to significant biases in simulating both TWS amplitude and long‐term trends (Rodell et al., 2018; Scanlon et al., 2018). Since 2002, the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) satellites, as the first and only mission capable of directly observing TWSC, have measured monthly changes in total water storage with unprecedented accuracy (Tapley et al., 2004). In contrast to traditional methods, GRACE observations represent changes in TWS caused by both anthropogenic and natural processes (Ahmed et al., 2014), and have made significant contributions to closing the water budget (Lehmann et al., 2022; Rodell & Reager, 2023; Tan et al., 2022), constraining water storage in hydrological models (Eicker et al., 2014; Gerdener et al., 2023; Girotto et al., 2016; Kumar et al., 2016; Li et al., 2019), identifying extreme events (Gerdener et al., 2020; Rodell & Li, 2023; Scanlon et al., 2022), and establishing the sea level budget (Cáceres et al., 2020; Velicogna et al., 2020). The GRACE mission operated from 2002 to 2017, with an 11‐month data gap between the end of GRACE (July 2017) and the start of its successor, GRACE Follow‐On (GRACE‐FO), in June 2018 (Landerer et al., 2020). This gap, along with the limited temporal coverage of GRACE, restricts its applicability in various climatic and hydrological studies, such as understanding historical extreme events (Kusche et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2016), constructing sea

level budgets (Dieng et al., 2017), and testing climate model simulations (Jensen et al., 2019, 2024), which require longer and uninterrupted TWSC time series beyond the GRACE period. To address this issue, previous studies have applied data‐driven methods, such as machine learning, to reconstruct long‐term TWSC outside the GRACE period (i.e., pre‐2002 and 2017–2018), by establishing empirical relationships between meteorological variables (e.g., precipitation and temperature) and GRACE data (Ahmed et al., 2019; Gyawali et al., 2022; Humphrey et al., 2017; Li et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2020, 2021). In this context, several reconstruction data sets of long‐term global TWSC beyond the GRACE period have been produced (Hacker & Kusche, 2024; Humphrey & Gudmundsson, 2019; Li et al., 2021; Yin et al., 2023). However, previous studies on extrapolating GRACE have mainly focused on the historical period, while many operational services—such as drought early warning (Hao et al., 2017) with the US Drought Monitoring and Forecast System (Monitor, 2014), or water and food security applications from the NASA Hydrological Forecast System (Arsenault et al., 2020)—require real‐time or even forecast products of GRACE‐like total water storage. GRACE‐derived TWSC is known to exhibit consistent delays in responding to climate variables such as precipitation and sea surface temperature (Humphrey et al., 2016; Li et al., 2024; Vishwakarma et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2020). This lag relationship allows for forecasting GRACE‐type total water storage changes several months in advance using pure observational products of climate variables as predictors. Notably, Reager et al. (2014) inferred flood potential several months ahead over the Mississippi River Basin using an autoregressive method based on this lag relationship. Li et al. (2024) developed an observation‐driven TWSC forecasting method by integrating the lag relationship into a machine learning‐based framework. However, the methodology framework was too complex for practical implementation in an operational forecasting system. To address this, Li et al. (2025) simplified the method to enhance its applicability in operational contexts, though their testing was limited to Europe. Despite these efforts, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have systematically evaluated the accuracy, reliability, and robustness of the observation‐driven TWSC forecasting method in global operational contexts, nor have they published GRACE‐like TWSC forecasting products that can be independently evaluated by others. Furthermore, the current GRACE‐FO mission still experiences a latency of a few months (approximately 2–3 months) in providing standard products. With this paper, we aim to address these shortcomings by forecasting GRACE data using machine learning, relying on the lag relationship of TWSC with hydrometeorological variables and indices. The primary objective of this paper is to develop an observation‐driven TWSC forecasting method suitable for use in an operational forecast system and to generate a global forecasting data set of GRACE‐like gridded total water storage for community testing. Our method is evaluated through a hindcast experiment, where forecasts are initialized monthly from December 2010 to April 2024, with lead times up to 12 months ahead of each initialized date. The data set is provided with four individual terms that make up the full TWSC signal, including seasonal cycles, linear trends, inter annuals, and sub‐seasonals, at a grid scale of one degree, covering the global land area (available at https://doi.pangaea.de/10.1594/PANGAEA.973113). Additionally, a semi‐operational data set (from January 2024 onward) based on this study, updated regularly or upon request, called the Global Land Water Storage Forecast Release 1 (GLWFC1.0), is publicly available (https://www.igg.uni‐bonn.de/apmg/de/data‐and‐models/grace‐fo‐forecasting). We anticipate this study will be relevant for drought early warning services (Bhardwaj et al., 2023; Cammalleri et al., 2021; Collins et al., 2016; Funk et al., 2019; Hao et al., 2017; Monitor, 2014; Otkin et al., 2015; Sharafi et al., 2021), sea level forecasting (Bahari et al., 2023; Nieves et al., 2021; Rus et al., 2023), the constraint of water storage forecasts using hydrological and land surface models (Arsenault et al., 2020; Cassagnole et al., 2021; Dion et al., 2021; Hauswirth et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025; Maxwell et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2022), forecasting loading corrections in GNSS and altimetry data analysis (Gerdener et al., 2023), forecasting Earth orientation parameters (Dill et al., 2019; Dobslaw & Dill, 2018), and addressing the latency issue in accessing standard GRACE TWSC products (Getirana et al., 2020).
2. Data and Methods
2.1. GRACE/‐FO Data
The GRACE/‐FO monthly mascon solutions from 2003 to 2024, produced by three institutions—the Center for Space Research (CSR) (Save et al., 2016), the Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) (Watkins et al., 2015), and NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center (GSFC) (Loomis et al., 2019)—are used as the target variables in the TWSC forecast (Table 1). The standard products of these mascon solutions are provided in equivalent water

thickness (units: cm), with correlated stripe errors intrinsically removed. Consequently, these products require no additional de‐striping or smoothing procedures. The three solutions exhibit slight variations due to differences in the processing algorithms and in the models used to correct for glacial isostatic adjustment (GIA). While the claimed spatial resolution of the mascon solutions is approximately three degrees, the standard products are provided at 0.25° or 0.5° grid scales globally. In this study, all three solutions were resampled to a common 1° grid using arithmetic grid averaging, ensuring consistency across data sets, and the forecasts were subsequently implemented on this unified grid. To ensure uninterrupted coverage over the entire period, the data gap between July 2017 and May 2018 between the two GRACE missions is filled using the reconstructed global TWSC data set produced by Yin et al. (2023), and any additional missing points in the GRACE/‐FO observations are interpolated. To interpolate missing GRACE/GRACE‐FO monthly observations, we first separated the seasonal and non‐seasonal components of the TWSC time series. The seasonal cycle was estimated from the complete portions of the original data and temporarily removed, leaving the non‐seasonal component. Missing months in the non‐seasonal series were then interpolated using a simple linear fitting approach, which also provided smoothing by reducing abrupt changes caused by data gaps. After interpolation, the previously extracted seasonal cycle was added back to the non‐seasonal series, restoring the original seasonal variability. This approach ensures continuity in the time series while preserving the realistic seasonal patterns.

2.3. Hydrological and Land Surface Model Products
2.3.1. GLDAS
The Global Land Data Assimilation System (GLDAS) (Rodell et al., 2004) integrates satellite‐ and ground‐based observational data with land surface models to estimate water and energy fluxes at the land surface. In this study, GLDAS‐NOAH model outputs from January 2010 to April 2024 are used to provide global mean TWSC estimates for comparison with GRACE/‐FO observations and forecast products.
2.3.2. GLWS2.0 (WGHM‐Based)
The Global Land Water Storage data set version 2.0 (GLWS2.0) is generated by assimilating GRACE/‐FO data into the WaterGAP Global Hydrological Model (WGHM) (Gerdener et al., 2023). GLWS2.0 provides estimates of global land water storage, including groundwater, soil moisture, snow, and other surface water components. In this study, we use the data set from January 2010 to December 2019 for comparison with GRACE/‐FO observations and the forecasted GRACE TWSC (GRACE‐FCast) products generated in this study.
2.3.3. ECMWF Forecast Products
The European Centre for Medium‐Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) provides forecast products of water storage from its fifth‐generation seasonal forecast system (SEAS5) (Johnson et al., 2019). SEAS5 simulates water storage across four soil layers (0–7 cm, 7–28 cm, 28–100 cm, 100–289 cm) with lead times up to 215 days. The land surface component of SEAS5 is based on the HTESSEL model (Orth et al., 2017), which represents vegetation, soil, snow, and open water compartments. Forecast products initialized from December 2009 to March 2024 are used here to benchmark GRACE‐FCast performance up to six months ahead.
2.4. Machine Learning Based TWSC Forecasting Method
2.4.1. Forward‐Moving Predictors
As systematically examined and illustrated in Figure 1 of Li et al. (2024), GRACE/‐FO total water storage responds to hydrometeorological variables with a delay of up to 12 months after removing linear and seasonal terms. This lag relationship enables us to forecast next year’s de‐seasoned and de‐linearized GRACE‐like total water storage changes using near real‐time observational forcing data as inputs. To apply this lag relationship in TWSC forecasting using a data‐driven method like machine learning, we start by decomposing both the employed GRACE/‐ FO TWSC and hydrometeorological variables into four components: (a) seasonal cycle, (b) linear term, (c) interannual, and (d) sub‐seasonal, based on the time series decomposition approach as developed by Li et al. (2020). Here we focus on forecasting the interannual and sub‐seasonal (abbreviated as “non‐seasonal” throughout the paper) terms of GRACE based on machine learning. The GRACE/‐FO linear trends often result from factors such as ice/glacier melt and human activities like dam management or reservoir management practices and water

withdrawals (Humphrey et al., 2017). Forecasting GRACE/‐FO linear trends requires a detailed approach that considers these factors and depending solely on hydrometeorological variables as inputs makes this task challenging. Assuming that linear trends in TWSC do not change significantly over short periods, such as from 1 year to the next, we predict GRACE/‐FO linear trends as well as the stationary seasonal cycles roughly by extrapolating from the previous year. These extrapolated GRACE/‐FO trends and seasonal terms are then integrated into the non‐seasonal forecasting to provide a full map of TWSC. As a caveat, extrapolating previous GRACE/‐FO seasonal terms and linear trends might introduce biases in certain cases, particularly where GRACE/‐FO linear trends are irregular due to factors such as inconsistent government policies on farming practices. Below, we describe how we prepare the optimal forward‐moving predictors for predicting non‐seasonal terms of TWSC. Here, it is important to highlight that we apply the following steps separately to the interannual and sub‐seasonal terms of hydrometeorological forcing data. This means that the non‐seasonal terms (interannual and sub‐seasonal) of TWSC are forecasted individually, rather than as a whole. First, we shift the non‐seasonal (either interannual or sub‐seasonal) terms of all hydrometeorological variables forward by one to 12 months along the timeline. In other words, we use predictors located in place B to forecast non‐seasonal TWSC in place A. This “spatial shifting” approach in identifying optimal predictors simply corresponds to the physics both of natural processes and anthropogenic transports that is, the hydrological cycle cannot be described well within a simple 1D vertical column. The correlation coefficients between the predictors and non‐seasonal GRACE/‐FO TWSC are significantly improved after applying this approach, particularly with larger forward‐moving lengths.
2.4.2. Long Short‐Term Memory (LSTM) Model
In this study, we employ the long short‐term memory (LSTM) method to perform the non‐seasonal TWSC forecast. LSTM, a variant of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), has memory cells that store and propagate information across time steps, enabling the capture of long‐term patterns in sequential data (Sherstinsky, 2020). This makes LSTM particularly effective for time series forecasting, as they can consider both immediate and distant past values. Previous studies have shown that LSTMs outperform conventional methods like Linear Regression (LR), Mulon (LR), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), and Support Vector Machine (SVM) in hydrological forecasting, such as streamflow prediction (Rahimzad et al., 2021). Here, the LSTM model is trained against the non‐seasonal GRACE/‐FO total water storage change and then used to forecast non‐seasonal terms of TWSC up to 1 year ahead, utilizing the optimal forward‐moving predictors identified in Section 2.4.1 as inputs. It is important to clarify again that, as described in Section 2.4.1, the predictors are identified individually at each grid point and for each forecasting lead time. This means that the optimal predictors are determined and used for training the LSTM model 12 times over a specific grid point, each time forecasting non‐seasonal TWSC for one of
12 lead times (Step 5 in Figure 1).
2.4.3. A Hindcast Experiment and Computational Flow
Here we conducted a hindcast experiment for the period from January 2010 to April 2024 to evaluate the developed forecasting method and generate a global forecast data set of GRACE‐like TWSC. Before running the experiment, certain parameters need to be initialized. As shown in Figure 1, the training period of LSTM is set from January 2003 to time t. The parameter t represents the end date of the training period and will be incremented by 1 month after each forecast iteration. The maximum forecast lead time is set to 12 months. This means that TWSC will be forecasted up to 12 months ahead of t in each forecast iteration. In this study, t is initially set to December 2009, making the year 2010 the first forecast period. Specifically, we will train LSTM on GRACE/‐FO data from January 2003 to December 2009 and then forecast the TWSC for the year 2010 in the initial forecast. Following this, t will be incremented by 1 month (i.e., t = t + 1), and the process of training and forecasting will be repeated for subsequent periods. After the initialization, we will then identify the optimal predictors and utilize

them for forecasting the non‐seasonal TWSC as described in Sections 2.3.1 and 2.3.2 (i.e., Steps two to five in
Figure 1). As we have mentioned, the seasonal and linear components of TWSC for each hindcast period are
forecasted by extrapolating the corresponding GRACE/‐FO terms from previous years and are added to the forecasted non‐seasonal TWSC to create a complete map of forecasted TWSC.
2.4.4. Uncertainty Estimates
For actual forecasting, it is essential to provide uncertainty estimates from the modeling process for each lead time, as no observations will be available to validate future forecasts. Our machine learning‐based method consists of two primary steps: training and forecasting. During the training phase, we will train the LSTM on non‐seasonal GRACE/‐FO data with the identified optimal predictors as forcing data. Once the model is trained, it can forecast TWSC using only the predictors as inputs. Typically, the performance of the model during the training phase reflects the reliability of its forecasting capability. 3. Results
3.1. The GRACE‐FCast TWSC Data Set Produced From the Hindcast Experiment
Using three different training GRACE/‐FO data sets (i.e., mascon solutions from CSR, JPL, and GSFC) and the identified forward‐moving predictors from eight types of hydrometeorological variables/indices (Table 1), we produce a total of three distinct GRACE‐FCast data sets (Table 2). The GRACE‐FCast data set provides total water storage changes (TWSC) in units of centimeters of equivalent water height, separated into four components: seasonal, linear trend, interannual, and sub‐seasonal, as well as the full map of TWSC. The interannual and sub‐seasonal components are forecasted by training the LSTM model against the GRACE/‐FO interannual and sub‐seasonal terms, while the full map integrates the LSTM‐forecasted non‐seasonal terms with the seasonal and linear terms, which are extrapolated from the corresponding GRACE/‐FO terms from previous years. For each data set, TWSC is forecasted up to 12 months ahead for each month from December 2009 to April 2024. These data sets are available at a monthly resolution and cover all 1‐degree grid cells of the global land area (including Greenland and Antarctica). 3.2. Evaluation With GRACE/‐FO Observations
Below, we evaluate the mean TWSC across three GRACE‐FCast data sets (i.e., JPL‐FCast, CSR‐FCast, and GSFC‐FCast) using the mean GRACE/GRACE‐FO mascons (from JPL, CSR, and GSFC) as validation. It is important to clarify that the GRACE/‐FO observations are used only for training the LSTM model. They are excluded from the forecast periods to ensure their independence when evaluating the GRACE‐FCast data sets produced in this study. Forecast performance is assessed using correlation coefficients (CC), root mean square error (RMSE), and mean relative error (MRE) at grid, basin, and global scales (Text S1 in Supporting Information S1). CC quantifies the temporal agreement between predicted and observed TWSC, reflecting the model’s ability to capture timing and variability, while RMSE measures the magnitude of absolute errors, indicating the overall deviation of predictions from observations. MRE accounts for the ratio of absolute error to the amplitude of TWSC, providing a normalized measure of error that is especially useful in regions with low TWSC variability.

Overall, GRACE‐FCast closely aligns with observed TWSC across most basins and lead times. This is likely because the forecasting method relies on extrapolating seasonal and linear components from past GRACE/‐FO observations, which performs well in regions where these signals persist over time. In contrast, the method shows reduced accuracy in basins with less stable hydrological patterns, including the Amur, Murray, and St. Lawrence. In these regions, fluctuations in seasonal cycles and long‐term trends are not well captured by simple extrapolation, leading to increased biases. This highlights a key limitation of the current forecasting approach: its limited ability to capture non‐stationary seasonal and long‐term dynamics.

4. Discussion and Conclusion

A central motivation of this work is to address the inherent latency in GRACE/‐FO TWSC products, which are typically released several months after observation, limiting their utility for operational and near‐real‐time applications, including drought early warning, water resource management, and hydrological forecasting. As evaluated by the GRACE observations, the TWSC forecasts generated by this machine learning‐based approach demonstrate robust performance at 1‐degree grid scale, with correlation coefficients exceeding 0.8 and RMSE below 4 cm in most regions, indicating strong temporal agreement with GRACE/‐FO observations. Despite this overall skill, certain regions—particularly arid and desert areas such as central Australia and the Sahara—exhibit reduced forecast accuracy. In these regions, the variability of TWSC is minimal, which leads to a low signal‐to‐noise ratio and makes it challenging for the model to accurately capture small but hydrologically significant fluctuations. To better characterize forecast performance in such low‐variability regions, we incorporated the MRE as an additional evaluation metric. Unlike standard measures such as RMSE or correlation, MRE accounts for the ratio of absolute error to the magnitude of observed variability, providing a more context‐sensitive assessment. The MRE also revealed higher relative errors in arid regions, highlighting the inherent limitations of predicting small anomalies under these conditions. These findings underscore the importance of interpreting TWSC forecasts with caution in dry regions. Comparisons with ECMWF SEAS5 forecasts and model simulations (GLDAS‐NOAH, WGHM‐GLWS2.0) reveal that the ML forecast consistently outperforms these products in capturing TWSC at both global and basin scales. While SEAS5 performs reasonably well for non‐seasonal TWSC forecasts over short lead times, it struggles to reproduce seasonal cycles and long‐term trends, limiting its suitability as a standalone GRACE surrogate. These findings underscore the unique value of data‐driven, observation‐based forecasts for extending GRACE‐like insights into the near future. In terms of forecasting extreme hydrological events, such as droughts, the non‐seasonal components of GRACE‐FCast show good potential, but the magnitude of extreme events is often underestimated. In addition, ML generates TWSC that are smoother than the satellite observations. This phenomenon arises from several interconnected factors. First, most ML models are optimized using loss functions such as mean squared error, which emphasize minimizing average deviations and lead models to approximate the conditional mean—thereby underrepresenting rare, high‐magnitude events. Extremes are also under‐sampled in training data, providing limited gradient information for the model to learn their dynamics. Regularization techniques (e.g., dropout, weight decay) and architectural features (e.g., convolutional filters, pooling) further encourage smooth mappings that suppress abrupt variations. Additionally, many hydrological extremes are driven by sub‐monthly processes or human activities not captured in the predictor set, making them inherently difficult for ML models to learn. The combined effect of these factors results in systematically attenuated peaks and damped variability in ML‐predicted TWS, leading to smoother outputs and an underestimation of hydrological extremes compared with GRACE observations. Overall, the observation‐driven forecasting method presents an opportunity for implementation in an operational TWSC forecasting system. The GRACE‐FCast data set generated here, along with regular updates to the new semi‐operational forecast data set (GLWFC1.0), will serve as an increasingly valuable resource for the scientific community and real‐time studies on drought and flood potential, constraining water storage in hydrological forecasting models, as well as for geodetic applications such as forecasting Earth orientation parameters and performing loading corrections in GNSS and altimetry data analysis. Additionally, this product provides an alternative solution to the latency issues associated with the current GRACE‐FO mission in delivering standard TWSC products.
